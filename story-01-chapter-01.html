19 March 2007
Back to Literature

At the crack of dawn, I again woke up to prepare for a series of wearying work days. Despite the rewarding salary, the work was repeated as the cycle of the four seasons. But it was the only way that helped sustain my existence, favourably making room for the spectrum of earthly flavours to infiltrate my life.

I was living in a room with an area of twelve square metres, not too spacious for me, but at least it had been my sanctuary for five years. The adornment of the room was rather mediocre, with a run-down bed, a black ebony desk, and a wardrobe smelling of soaked soil after a rainfall. In addition to these monotonous pieces of furniture, I also brought back a potted ivy placed next to the window facing the Puerto to dilute the stale atmosphere in the room. Admittedly, that made sense anyway. Normal people like me could hardly have free time.

I took some regular exercise to warm up and had boring breakfast with cereal and milk only. After that, I put on the uniform and headed straight to the Puerto. I had been digging my own grave in this place by working at sea and fishing with other men whose age was the same as that man’s, the man, whose blood and flesh I was carrying, was disgusting, and I had no intention to see him ever again.

There had been a few young men who were the same age as me, but they were superficial and resembled water puppets manipulated to do this and that, merely to go through the motions of life. Although I tried to get along with them, my effort was completely in vain. Thus, I decided to act more nonchalantly, focusing on the work and waiting for the salary to come—all of it was for my future studies.

Over the course of time, the way to the Puerto had changed in a nauseous manner. For five years, I had been witnessing everything being modernised so much that it left me overwhelmed. I first came here when I was eighteen and, indeed, a young boy who was learning to grow found himself engulfed by strangeness of the world outside his hometown. I was astonished with what I had experienced and cried every night because of the homesickness.

It was my first year here after all. It had been a long time since I cried. Verily, I wanted to cry. I even immersed myself into heartbreak novels that were lauded on some forums by netizens, but it did not affect me even a little bit. They were depressing, but were not powerful enough to have me in tears.

I arrived at the Puerto at seven. I checked the fishing equipment, had a chat with the men, and set sail. The weather was pleasant today. The sun at the far east was emerging from the curtain of the sea. It gave off a light that, to many people, was warm and gentle, melting their frozen soul and mind. But to me, it was a pure light that exposed the darkest corners within me. I hastily turned away when a shaft of sunlight illuminated me.

Our ship berthed at noon. Whilst we were having a rushed lunch, our captain introduced a newcomer. I heard that he was my age and had certain experience at sea before. I did not think much but kept eating to go back to work.

We set sail again at one fifteen. After greeting the seniors, he came to me and said: “Nice to meet you. I’m Pedro. I heard you’re around my age.” He held out his hand.

“Nice to meet you too,” I said in a flat tone.

“You’ve been working here for years, right? I hope you’ll go easy on me.” He smiled, his white teeth gleaming.

“You flatter me.”

I turned to a flock of terns flying high above the sky. Seeing how carefree they looked, I bet they were at peace with the world. At times, I had wished to be one of their kind, maybe an owl for instance, to fly around, and whenever I felt tired, I could perch on a random branch and take a break. But then I realised that if I were any animal, I would be very likely to fall into a relentless hunt of humans or other beings. That cycle literally made me sick. Then I did not want to become birds anymore. I wanted to be an insentient stone, unbound from the reincarnation wheel.

Sensing my silence, he attempted to break the ice and said: “The sky’s lovely, isn’t it?”

“It is.”

“But soon, the storm will approach,” he said in a more serious tone.

“I’m used to it.” I had long since grown accustomed to this irregularity, variable weather: the sun would shine in the morning, and the rain would fall in the afternoon. In all honesty, I was amazed at his weather forecast. He must have had the experience before, so observing and predicting the weather was alright to him. I wondered whether he was not one of those water puppets.

No sooner had the rain started pouring than we hid ourselves into the cabin. It happened at around four, and thanks to the prediction of the men, our ship was not too far away from the shore. I was sipping a beer, whilst the others were talking about women, gambling, and their family affairs. I sometimes had no idea why they were so reckless and, most importantly, indulgent. But the thought soon went away, as it was their lives, and I clearly had no right to meddle.

Pedro stepped towards me. He took a puff, leaned against the bulkhead, stared at me, and smiled, saying: “Are you an alcoholic?”

“It seems so. Want a sip?”

He nodded, taking a sip and exclaiming: “You’re a heavy drinker.”

“I wanna try your fag.”

“Are we trading?” He frowned.

“Kinda.”

I took a drag. The smoke seeped into my lungs, somewhat warming my slightly shaking body. “Thank you.”

“You take it. I have plenty more.”

I held the fag onto my right hand and the beer on my left. For a moment, I was no better than the addicts shown on television. Now I had a feeling as if my human defense was being eroded until it vanished into nothingness.

“What was your previous job?”

“I went fishing like this, but the boss there was like a dickhead.”

“How come?”

“That was the payday, but he said nothing and just stiffed us half of ours. He made up an excuse that we caught low quality fish, and he couldn’t sell them. ‘That’s the rule,’ he said. But we didn’t do such a thing. We worked like a dog to meet the deadline, and all he could do was give such a dumb excuse.”

“He’s literally a dickhead.”

“That’s the story. I’m done with him, and here I am.”

“Don’t worry about the pay. Everything’s fine here. You just work to death instead.”

He laughed. “I still don’t know your name.”

“I’m Anthony.”

“You don’t look local.”

“I’m Vietnamese. You don’t look local either.”

“I’m Spanish.”

“I know, señor.” I teasingly smiled, curtsying.

“How?” He asked, his eyes widening.

“By your name and the way you pronounce words.”

“Sí, sí,” he replied. “But I didn’t know you’re Vietnamese.”

“Never mind. My look is confusing.” I downed all the beer and smoked the fag.

The ship docked, and we safely returned. Because of the unavoidable incident, we were allowed to be off work sooner than usual. Before I left, Pedro had asked me to hang out with him, so he could familiarise himself with the new place. I was hesitant at first, but I quickly turned him down. It was not because I was selfish, but rather because I was not acquainted with associating with male peers regularly.

I went back to my room and lay down the creaky bed. “Home sweet home!” When I drifted off, that scene, all of a sudden, came back to me, the day I decided to leave my family’s embrace.

It was not because I wanted to grow up, but because I had to. That man, who had abused the family from the time I was just a foetus until I finished high school, felt no rue—not even a slight bit. He was always bad-tempered and behaved as if the world were ending. I sometimes referred to him as a mad dog, as I did not know when he would get mad and turn on my mother and me.

Speaking of my mother, I felt so helpless. She had endured his emotional instability since she married him. Many times, she was physically abused, berated, and vehemently humiliated, but she quickly forgave for what he did and continued the torment. I wondered if it was the nature of great tolerance of the Vietnamese towards the unpleasantness or it was mere unconditional tolerance of my mother. I did not know. But there was one thing I was sure that I really loved my mother. As long as my breath still lasted, I would not let anyone hurt my mother ever again. I did not want my mother to be sad or cry anymore. I simply wanted her to be happy, no more than that.

After showering, I studied for a while. I was researching English literature, its development, writing styles, as well as changes related to literary genres. If everything went according to plan, I would be studying at Southampton University with a decent scholarship. This signified that I was to stop this job or, at best, I would work less frequently than before. Even so, I had to have my own plan for my future.

Phoning my mother and my grandmother, I came back to bed and prayed. I did not know why I was so devoted like this. It must have been that man who made me find solace in Buddha, in the hope that may him bring felicity and peace to my mother and my grandmother. In the prayers, I also adjured him to show me where was my man. I clasped hands in prayer, kneeling facing east, where the sun was gradually fading away.

Being sober meant I had to come back to the reality. I no longer underwent the tipsy effect. Mindlessly, I thought of Pedro whilst staring at the Puerto. He, for a moment, made me view him differently. “He’s rather… fine.”

The moon was absent tonight. I opened the window, lit a fag, and took a deep puff, my eyes heavily steeped in the tangle of that day.
