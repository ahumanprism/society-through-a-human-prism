23 July 2014

← Back to literature

I got up at five. Then I sat at the desk and prepared for my next novel. My first one was published last autumn and received many positive reviews from readers. To be fair, I did have a steadfast readership. Aside from writing, my day job was still at this Puerto.

I lit a fag and looked towards the port. I thought after years of studies, my life could have gone differently. But in fact, my spirit had seemed to have an indistinct link with this fishing business. I remembered that when I got interviewed about my first publication, there was someone asking why I kept doing this manual labour, but not some white-collar work. I chuckled and said: “What’s the difference?”

Honestly, I did not prefer to be restricted, so white-collar work was not my goal. At least I gained a certain amount of experience with fishing and was promoted by the boss, so the workload now was not too much for me to handle. I, many times, did little but made a bundle, which made me slightly embarrassed. For that reason, I intentionally deducted a portion of my salary to regale the men with drinks at some point during a month so as to pay tribute to their enthusiasm towards me over the years.

I heard that he planned to wed her in spring. In the message, as I recalled, he reminded me to stay professionally formal presiding over their wedding. I had always known this day would come. But no matter how hard I tried to tell myself that everything was going to be alright, my tears just fell silently. I did be able to see him happy with someone, but that did not mean my heart was willing to do so. On many nights, it screamed out his name, as if it wanted to hold him back, disinclined to let him settle down and be happy with someone else but me. My reason, then again, told me that instead of being so down, I should hope that he lived well. With that, both my heart and my reason could finally cure this illness of the mind in peace.

He was meant to go home today, so I managed to get off work soon and readily awaited his motorbike’s roar. His mother and his sister were going to be there too. I had not met them for a while, so I did not know how to behave suitably. To show my gratitude, I had prepared some handmade biscuits and apple juice for them. In addition, I had no plan to stay over at his house or, more precisely, at his room. If I lingered there for even a moment, my heart would not bear such a pain.

Seeing me writing on my tod during lunch break, the boss came to converse with me: “Your writing is good!”

Surprised, I said: “Have you read my works?”

He nodded. “Though I don’t know much about romance, the words have swayed me in part.”

“Thank you.”

“By the way, Pedro told me he would come back to work here.”

“Yeah. He told me that already.”

“You lot are literally inseparable. But he’s a man now, and he’s getting his own home soon. Time really flies, doesn’t it?”

“He’s… a man now,” I stammered, directing my gaze to the distant sea.

He patted me on the shoulder and said: “What about you? When will be your turn to have your own home?”

“I still love being alone.”

“Oh? Or did you have someone on your mind, and you’re not going to tell me, young pen?”

“I did,” I told myself. My favourite was Pedro. He was born on 07 April, 1984. His hobby was making others smile. He was talkative, but after joining the navy, his mouth appeared humble. He liked almost everything and hated bullies. He loved paella, his home food, and he was very passionate about espresso. He told me that he could down all three cups of espresso in the morning solely to stay awake.

Back then, he did not care about art stuff, like paintings, movies, or music. Yet, when he absorbed my enlightenment, he had become fascinated about them. I once taught him to play the guitar, but he said the strings were harsh. I once taught him to sketch me, but the final work was grotesquely unhinged. I once showed him several poignant novels and science-fiction movies, but he fell asleep halfway all the time.

Classic Pedro. He was practical, because he experienced earthly flavours from a very young age. He had bad habits, but in my eyes, they were psychological scars that were difficult to mend. I even noted down all details about him in my own notebook and kept it safe. Height, weight, and even the penis’s length, which was unnecessarily included, were all recorded to monitor his well-being. I sometimes felt like his private nurse, and I loved doing so. That said, he turned my prism into something vivid and alive.

Night slowly descended. I had prepared everything and was sitting on the step waiting for him. “How wonderful the stars are,” I whispered, hand resting on the chin. It had been a while since I last gazed at these blinking pearls in the sky. They looked so carefree, but merely gleamed at night and then faded away. I wondered whether a transient life like that is worthy of living.

His motorbike’s roar echoed from afar. I hurried to step forward as if I were scared of being unnoticed. The moment he saw me, he rushed to hug me and softly said: “Ant.”

I amazedly answered: “What’s wrong?”

“Nothing. It’s just a greeting hug.”

“Feel free, Caba. But are you tired of traveling so far? Do you want to come in and lie down for a little bit?”

“Vale. It’s still early anyway. I want to lie down on your ancient bed.”

We laughed. After getting in, I lit him a fag and asked: “Give me a twirl to see if you’re doing fine.”

He did what I said. During days not having met each other, he even grew bigger than before. His shoulders were so broad that they could hide me from the clamorous crowd or that they could be like age-old trees offering me shades. Sadly, I would never be able to reach for those experiences. At least, nevertheless, he was still doing fine.

“Have you spotted any missing flesh, Ant?”

I pretended to stroke the chin contemplatively and said: “Everything is still alright.”

“Your turn. Give me a twirl then.”

“Me?” I felt loath but still did to gratify him. I was not sure if I had a balanced diet. In recent years, I had been continuously skipping breakfast, and sometimes merged breakfast and lunch into one. To stave off hunger, I simply drank water or sugar-free milk. But if I did not tell him, he would never know anything about this.

“You look like… a twig.”

“Just because you’re bigger than me doesn’t mean that I’m small.”

“Oh yeah?” He suddenly lifted me off the ground and put me down on the bed, saying: “You’re really small, you know.”

“What the fuck!” I exclaimed, as he picked me up. Our eyes met, for a moment, and they were so close that I thought they were going to melt into each other. I even felt his puffing breaths, puissant and tense, like a beast playing with its prey. Whilst he was distracted, I turned the tables on him and flipped him over, continuing: “But being small has its own strengths. Anyway, I have an idea. Can we go easy on each other? My bed can’t stand your weight.”

He smiled and said: “Okay, but I want to get some sleep.”

“Are you feeling unwell?”

“Sort of tired. Let me call my mother and tell her I’m going home late.”

I rubbed his head and said: “Have a rest then. When you feel better, we’ll go to see your mother.”

“Thank you and sorry.”

“For what?”

“I should have been in a good mood to see you, not this gloomy face.”

“You don’t have to, Caba. No matter how happy or sad you are, I’ll respect you and be with you. Don’t think too hard. Go to sleep!”

I sat at the desk and carried on my side job. His sleeping expression was so delightful. However, I knew that this scene would end sooner or later. So be it. I should cherish the present and tried to move on. Even if I cried or moaned, would anyone care? I chose to be an outcast. I would never regret. But if I did, I would, at the beginning, since I was cognisant of the fact that my existence was monochrome, like a flashback, neither here nor there, but redundant and ignominious.

His sleep-talking always made me giggle. This time, he was talking about food and drinks whilst I was struggling to hold back my laughter, face turning red. And then, he fell back asleep as if nothing ever happened. Come to think of it, we were already thirty. On his thirtieth birthday, I specially knit him a blanket—or a wedding blanket for both of them. It took me about three months to get it done. As I knew he got bigger, knitting extra was better for the bride.

“Ant, please. Ant, are you alright?” He called my name drowsily.

I came to bed and held his hand tightly, saying: “Caba, what’s wrong? I’m here.” I kept calling his name to help him get out of some nightmare. I grabbed a hanky to wipe the beads of sweat from his forehead. “Caba, wake up!”

“I’m so sorry, Ant.”

“You did nothing wrong. Caba, wake up.”

“That happened to you… because of me.”

In that moment, I was stupefied. The wound now was torn asunder again. Over time, I had to battle the nightmares about that day, the day I was besmeared. It disgusted me so much that I had to stay up all night to avoid being lost in a maze of those foul memories. But he did not think so. He thought that he was the root of everything. It was because of him. If he had not invited me to go there, I might have been happy, unruffled to enjoy literature, or at least at peace with the world. He thought that he was the one not only making me happy, but indirectly pushing me to the vulgar hell. He thought of himself badly perpetually.

Then he woke up. We, awash with tears, hugged each other. He told me that the nightmare was so frightening and begged me to stay. I reassured him and decided to sleep with him, so that he could feel less guilty. I caressed his hair, and we fell asleep shortly after that. Still, my tears remained on my cheeks.
